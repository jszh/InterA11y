It is a very good question and answer site. I know the question at hand is “what is it?”, but I'll add my likes and dislikes because I think they go toward the experience of it, at least from my perspective.

The things I like about Quora are:

The people. There are some amazing writers who share their thoughts and perspectives.

The diversity. The number and variety of perspectives you see on a question is often impressive. Views I never would have come up with on my own, whether I like them or not.

A sense of genuineness. For the most part, people say what they think and seem to be themselves, whether they think you're great, or full of crap. Most of the time, they're trying to be helpful.

A very loose sense of togetherness. Over the past year or two, I have gotten familiar with certain writers through their writing. I haven't met any of them directly, but I feel like I know them. In the same amount of time, I have begun seeing some of the same people asking me questions and commenting on my answers. It's a nice sense of “community”.

The things I don't like about Quora are:

Trolls. Those who write idiotic questions just to get others to waste their time and energy on answering them are ridiculous. I see good people get sucked into this quite a bit. It's a shame. Thank goodness for the ability to see everything a user asks and answers via their profile (under question details and scrolling until you get to the bottom). Block and mute are vital tools.

Memes. Quora didn't have this until recent years, now it does. Like other social media, if you don't take active control over what you see via block and mute and the like, you probably won't enjoy it. After a while, you'll get tired of it or bored, and then leave.

Bot questions. Quora has generated some decent questions via their bot. But for me, if the question doesn't come from a person, I'm not interested. You can block/mute the question generator.

Quora is where facts go to stretch, truth goes to die, and egos go to inflate beyond all earthly proportion.

It’s a platform allegedly built for sharing knowledge — but more often serves as a global soapbox for self-proclaimed geniuses, part-time philosophers, and people who once read a headline and now consider themselves thought leaders.

Ask a question like “What’s the best way to invest $1,000?” and you’ll get 12 contradictory responses from:

  • a crypto bro with zero financial disclosures,
  • Someone who believes gold bars under the mattress are the only safe future,
  • and a monk who tells you to invest in inner peace, then links to his eBook.

Quora thrives on the illusion of expertise. Want legal advice from someone whose only courtroom experience is binge-watching Law & Order? They’re answering right now — in all caps.

But it’s not just the answers. Oh no. The questions themselves are a descent into the surreal:

  • “How do I become immortal without paying for it?”
  • “Could I beat a wolf in a fistfight if I had a can of tuna?”
  • “Is Elon Musk watching me through my toaster?”

Meanwhile, Quora’s moderation is like a referee who shows up halfway through the brawl, shrugs, and says, “Looks fine to me.”

And then there’s the “answer requests.” Nothing like being summoned by a stranger who thinks you’re qualified to explain quantum mechanics because one time you mentioned you liked The Big Bang Theory.

In short, Quora is a carnival of misplaced confidence, humblebrags disguised as wisdom, and unsolicited life stories no one asked for.

But hey, where else can you watch someone argue with a PhD about the shape of the Earth — and almost win?
