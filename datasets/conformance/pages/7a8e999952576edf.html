The 2024 Carbon-Border Levy, Explained

By Dana Olusanya · Updated June 14, 2024 · 9 min read

Brussels spent the better part of a decade arguing about how to stop heavy industry from simply relocating to countries with weaker emissions rules. The answer that finally emerged is a tariff that travels with the carbon, not the company. Importers of steel, cement, aluminium, fertiliser and electricity must now surrender certificates priced to match what a European producer would have paid under the bloc’s The EU ETS is a “cap-and-trade” market: a shrinking cap sets the total tonnes of CO₂ that covered installations may emit, and they buy or sell allowances within it. The carbon-border levy pegs import charges to this price. . The mechanism is meant to level the field without handing domestic firms a windfall.

Critics on both flanks have lined up against it. Trade ministries in several developing economies call the levy a green tariff in disguise, one that exports the cost of Europe’s climate ambition onto exporters who never had a vote in setting it. Industry groups inside the bloc, meanwhile, warn that the free allowances they currently receive will be clawed back faster than the new border charge can protect them. The result, they argue, is a transition that squeezes from both directions at once and leaves the most exposed manufacturers carrying the gap.

Whether the policy survives its first full year may turn less on principle than on paperwork. Customs authorities must verify embedded-emissions figures supplied by thousands of foreign producers, many of whom have never measured their footprint to the standard the regulation demands. Auditors expect a wave of disputes.
